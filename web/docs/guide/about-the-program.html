About the NYS Collaborative Care Medicaid Program

What the program is, how it began, what it asks of practices, and where the metrics in this guide fit. Everything here comes from New York State documents, listed at the end with their dates. Where they differ, the newest wins.

This guide is an independent project. It isn't published or endorsed by the Office of Mental Health (OMH). Questions about the program, billing or reporting go to OMH at NYSCollaborativeCare@omh.ny.gov.

Collaborative Care

Collaborative Care (CoCM) treats common mental health conditions, such as depression and anxiety, in primary care. Three people share each patient's care:

  • the primary care provider, who stays the patient's main clinician;
  • a behavioral health care manager (BHCM), who leads day-to-day care: screening, engagement, brief evidence-based treatment, follow-up and medication support;
  • a psychiatric consultant, who reviews the caseload with the BHCM at least weekly, focusing on patients who aren't improving, and recommends changes to treatment.

The team tracks every patient in a registry and measures symptoms routinely with scales such as the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, changing treatment when a patient isn't improving. OMH describes the model through five principles: a patient-centered care team, population-based care, measurement-based treatment to target, evidence-based care, and accountability.

How the program began

  • 2013–2014: a NYS Department of Health medical home grant program set up Collaborative Care in academic medical centers.
  • 2015: OMH launched the Collaborative Care Medicaid Program to sustain that work. New York was the first state to pay for Collaborative Care through Medicaid. In 2016, 36 sites took part.
  • 2018: CMS began paying for Collaborative Care, and commercial plans followed, so it's now reimbursable across payers in New York.
  • 2026: OMH reports more than 430 primary care practices providing Collaborative Care and eligible for Medicaid reimbursement.

What the program asks of a practice

To bill Medicaid for Collaborative Care, a practice needs prior approval from the Commissioners of Health and of Mental Health, through an application to OMH. Primary care practices can apply, licensed under Article 28 or not. A practice commits to:

  • a clinic implementation team: trained primary care providers, BHCMs, a psychiatric consultant, and financial and data leads;
  • a registry that tracks each patient's care and symptom scores, supports case review, and can produce the data OMH asks for;
  • a written workflow that follows the five principles, from screening to discharge;
  • quarterly data reports to OMH.

How billing works, code by code, is in NYS Medicaid billing.

Each month a patient is billed, the care team must have at least one clinical contact with them, in which treatment is delivered and documented, and at least one completed symptom scale. The BHCM must also consult the psychiatric consultant for an hour or more each week about enrolled patients.

How it's paid

As of April 2026:

  • Collaborative Care is billed monthly, directly to fee-for-service Medicaid. It's carved out of Medicaid managed care, so this applies to managed care members too.
  • The monthly base rate (procedure codes T2022, 99492 and 99493) is $177.64, or $189.18 in hospital clinics. OMH marks these rates as pending approval; see Medicaid billing.
  • One add-on per month (99494, rate code 5261) pays $88.81, or $94.59 in hospital clinics, when the team spends at least 30 minutes beyond the base requirement.
  • A patient can receive up to 24 months of Collaborative Care, not necessarily in a row. The limit resets after six months without a service.
  • The earlier rule that withheld part of each payment until a patient improved ("retainage") has ended.

The data OMH asks for

Approved practices report aggregate numbers to OMH each quarter, through REDCap survey links that OMH sends. The numbers are:

  • enrollment, Medicaid enrollment and new enrollment;
  • average duration of treatment;
  • the rate of patients with a clinical contact;
  • improvement and remission;
  • psychiatric case review;
  • the practice's depression screening rate and screening yield.

These are the eleven metrics in OMH's 2025 metrics document, each defined for a month. This guide counts them month by month. How three months become a quarterly submission depends on what OMH's REDCap form asks for (see open questions). This guide explains how to count each one, and the calculator counts them from a practice's own data.
